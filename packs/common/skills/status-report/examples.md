# Show what moved the investigation

A useful update gives the next reader something to think with. These examples show
possible shapes, not tasks to repeat or sections to fill. Choose the shape that
fits what you actually learned, or write it another way. Let the substance decide
how much room it needs.

## A surprise opens another question

> The unexpected feature is [observation]. It suggests [possible mechanism], though
> [alternative explanation] is still plausible. [Artifact] contains the evidence.
> A useful next experiment would distinguish those explanations by [changed condition].

The contribution is the new distinction. Keep the experiment or derivation beside
it, with the detail needed to assess whether the distinction is real.

## A roadblock becomes more precise

> This approach needs [assumption], and [evidence] shows where that assumption fails.
> The obstruction applies to [scope]. It leaves [different representation or route]
> open because that route does not require [assumption]. The unresolved step is [question].

A failed route can teach us how to choose another. Explain what the evidence closes
and what it leaves undecided; there is no need to manufacture either hope or finality.

## A connection changes the approach

> Viewed as [another structure], the difficult step resembles [a neighboring problem].
> The correspondence preserves [important feature] but loses [other feature].
> [Small construction or argument] suggests a way forward. The analogy still needs
> work at [specific point].

The useful contribution is the translation and its limits. Follow the connection
far enough to make it inspectable. A familiar name alone is not an explanation.

## Several possibilities become a better choice

> [Route A] exposes [mechanism], while [route B] could bypass [obstruction].
> The evidence in [artifact] makes [next question] especially interesting. [Uncertainty]
> is what could change that judgment.

Keep alternatives alive when they have substance. This need not become a ranking
exercise or a checklist of routes; it can be one clear paragraph about where the
investigation could go and why.

## A correction makes the claim more useful

> The evidence supports [precise claim] under [conditions]. My earlier interpretation
> also assumed [extra step], which [new reasoning] does not justify. The narrower
> claim suggests [new question or consequence]. Full argument: [artifact].

Link to the earlier evidence and preserve any dissent. The manager can edit the
contribution or archive it when a clearer account takes its place. Git keeps the
previous wording; the report need not repeat the whole history.

## A tool limitation becomes actionable feedback

> [Limitation] blocked [specific investigation]. The useful distinction appears to be
> [condition], as shown in [minimal fixture]. [Improvement] would let us explore
> [currently inaccessible question]. Meanwhile, [independent avenue] remains useful.

This can be a short Markdown contribution or a line in FEEDBACK.md. A decision that needs the operator
belongs in the question inbox, where its answer can be kept with the question.

## Give substantial work its room

A complete proof, an unfamiliar derivation, a reproducible exploit, a protocol
trace, a substantial experiment, or an independent review may need a large artifact.
Keep the detail. A short opening and links can help readers enter at the relevant
point. Remove repeated background where it adds nothing; preserve assumptions,
evidence, dissent, and the reasoning that makes the result checkable.

A worker's answer can simply hand over the useful artifact and explain what is
interesting about it. The manager decides what merits promotion to the shared
record. There is no expectation of a new note or report for every worker answer.
