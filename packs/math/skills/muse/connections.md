# Representations and unexpected relatives

Read the statement fresh. Separate its quantifiers, domain, hypotheses, and intended conclusion. Which hypothesis carries the weight? Can it be weakened, replaced, or made explicit? A failed proof is a fact about that argument, not automatically the theorem.

Change the representation: a recurrence into an operator; a combinatorial count into a generating function; a geometric constraint into duality; a discrete process into an energy or potential; a local obstruction into a global invariant. Write the correspondence and the information it loses.

Try the smallest cases, a degenerate limit, an extremal example, and a deliberately adversarial construction. A counterexample to a stronger claim may suggest the right weaker statement. A special case can reveal a mechanism without proving the general theorem.

Look across algebra, topology, probability, analysis, optimization, and theoretical computer science for the same shape of difficulty. Compare compactness with finite witnesses, conservation with invariants, symmetry with quotienting, and uncertainty with entropy. Analogies become useful when their assumptions are checked.

Physics may suggest a variational principle, scaling law, phase transition, or conserved quantity. Computer science may suggest an algorithm, adversary, representation cost, or reduction. Turn the suggestion into a mathematical statement before treating it as evidence.

Try two proof routes that fail for different reasons. Can one route supply the missing lemma of the other? Work backward from the desired conclusion. Search historical proofs and primary sources for an obstruction that was bypassed rather than beaten directly.

Use computation to discover structure: exact arithmetic, symbolic checks, small exhaustive examples, numerical experiments with recorded precision. Save the script and inputs. Computation can expose a conjecture or disprove a universal statement; a finite search alone does not prove one.

Revisit a dismissal without inheriting its verdict. Ask a fresh reader to examine the source and strongest plausible claim first, then compare with the old objection. What did the second reading notice? What would another patient attempt try? Keep paper-specific questions and named obstructions in the lab’s own idea bank.
