# Randomness as a change of viewpoint

Sometimes a rigid object becomes easier to study as one member of a family.
Instead of constructing the desired object directly, ask what a distribution over
candidates would need to favor, avoid, or preserve. The distribution is part of
the argument; choosing it can be the creative step.

A naive random construction may fail because it ignores a constraint. Could a
conditional construction preserve that constraint automatically? Could independent
choices be replaced by a correlated family that suppresses the troublesome
interaction? Conversely, adding a small random perturbation can reveal which
features are stable and which rely on exact coincidence.

Average behavior and existence are different from typical behavior. An average
can suggest that a useful object exists without showing that ordinary samples
will find it. A rare configuration can dominate an average. Inspect the proposed
logical step before turning an attractive simulation into a claim.

There is a converse direction too: a deterministic example may be compressing a
probabilistic mechanism. Ask what it samples, balances, or cancels. Understanding
that mechanism might suggest other constructions with the same property, or show
why the original example is unusually fragile.

For an exploratory computation, choose observables that distinguish possible
mechanisms. Preserve seeds, the sampling rule, and the conditions under which an
observation appears. If the pattern changes under another distribution, ask what
that change teaches about the geometry of the candidate space. A useful outcome
may be a new conjecture about the exceptional set rather than a verdict on the
original statement.
