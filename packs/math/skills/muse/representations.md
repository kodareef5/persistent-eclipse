# Let another representation do the work

A representation is useful when it makes a difficult operation simple while
preserving what matters. A suggestive resemblance becomes an argument only when
the translation can be followed in both directions, or when its one-way nature
is understood.

Imagine a problem stated as a sequence of local moves. Could a graph turn its
history into reachability? Could an algebraic encoding turn composition into
multiplication? Could a potential assign each move a measurable change? These
translations expose different things: reachability, cancellation, conservation,
or monotonicity. Choose the feature that addresses the obstruction rather than
collecting descriptions of the same example.

Try changing the object being studied. A family of examples may be easier to
understand through the transformations between them. A difficult function may be
more revealing through its level sets. A collection of constraints may become
clearer through the space of objects satisfying them. Ask what becomes visible
in the new object that the original description concealed.

An analogy with another field can begin with a small dictionary: objects,
operations, constraints, and the question being asked. Then find a place where
the dictionary breaks. A translation that preserves addition may not preserve
order; one that preserves local structure may lose a global obstruction. The
break is often more informative than the initial resemblance.

A useful artifact is a worked translation of one difficult step, with its lost
information stated nearby. It can be brief or substantial. The interesting next
question is whether the translated step can return something stronger, simpler,
or unexpectedly different to the original problem.
