# The assumptions we brought with us

An obstruction may belong to the object, to a representation, or to the method we
chose. Separating those possibilities can turn an apparently exhausted problem
into a different question.

Start with the exact sentence the argument needs. “The inverse exists” might mean
an inverse on the whole space, a left inverse on a reachable subspace, or merely a
way to recover one observable. Those are different demands. Which one does the
original problem actually make? A failure of the strongest demand may say little
about the weakest.

Look at a step where information disappears. Taking an absolute value discards a
sign; averaging discards variation; passing to a quotient identifies objects;
choosing coordinates may hide a symmetry. Sometimes that loss is what makes the
problem tractable. Sometimes the discarded information contains the missing
invariant. Try carrying just that information through a smaller version of the
argument and see what changes.

A useful miniature might have two states, one exceptional boundary, or the first
noncommuting pair. Choose it to isolate the suspected obstruction. Ask whether the
failure survives when unrelated complexity is removed. If it does, the miniature
may explain the mechanism; if it vanishes, ask which interaction restored it.

There is also a constructive direction: instead of asking whether an assumption
can be removed, ask what weaker conclusion survives without it. An approximate
statement, a restricted domain, or a classification of exceptions may expose the
structure needed for the original claim. Keep clear which conclusion was actually
established and which remains an invitation.
