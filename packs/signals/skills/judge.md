# Judge

Use this when leads, explanations, or plans compete and no single test settles the
choice. For one factual claim, pe-challenger and pe-layered-challenge already help.
Start with the comparison that matters to the mission. A short argument may be enough.

## Find the difference that matters

Read the candidates against SCOPE and the operator's priorities. Check their factual
premises, then compare the reasoning: what does each explain, assume, leave out, or
need to be true? Give the strongest alternative a fair hearing. An unusual idea
should not lose merely because the familiar one is easier to write convincingly.

Say which direction you favor and why, the strongest objection, and what could
change the choice. Sometimes the useful answer is that the options trade off, the
evidence is insufficient, or each works under different conditions. No forced
ranking, invented score, or standard verdict vocabulary is required.

## Disagreement is material to investigate

Keep who reached which conclusion and on what evidence. A lab or model name is
provenance, not authority. Find the assumption, observation, or priority behind the
split. Ask whether the difference is real or comes from different dates, definitions,
or information. Test the hinge when possible; forecast or watch it when useful.

Do not vote, average, or keep asking until everyone agrees. Record what further
work established, including a mistake and its correction. Keep remaining dissent
attributed and inspectable. A plausible minority view gives the lab somewhere to
look when the favorite fails. Preference is not refutation: a negation note needs
actual counterevidence or a supported barrier.

## More scrutiny when it helps

For a consequential or stubborn comparison, try a fresh independent take, an allowed
different model, or a researcher arguing for the neglected alternative. Give them
the mission, scope, evidence, and question without priming them with the winner.
Use the operator's permitted models, effort, slots, and budget.

If presentation seems to drive the judgment, try removing author names, comparing
similar-length accounts, or reversing their order. A changed answer is a reason
to inspect the reasoning, not proof that the options are equal. A few plausible
futures can expose where a plan breaks. These are techniques to choose from, not
mandatory rounds or a council every decision must staff.

## Keep moving

Before another review, ask what useful information it could add. Go after a missing
fact, try an overlooked route, or decide provisionally and name the condition for
revisiting it. Keep alternatives worth returning to and the attempts already made.
Give the freed capacity another worthwhile question; repeating the same debate
with the same evidence is not deeper thought.
