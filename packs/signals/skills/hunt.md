# Hunt

Read MISSION and SCOPE. PE supplies the research record and collaboration rules;
{{note:records}} maps a desk's work onto them. The operating desks ({{note:desks}})
and starter configurations ({{note:starting}}) help turn a broad mandate into work
the operator can actually use.

## Setting up

Carry the operator's objective, access and resource limits, and delivery expectations
into SCOPE. Start with a reasonable reading of the request; ask when a missing detail
would materially change the work. A broad mandate leaves room to choose the research.
Choose initial sources and get going. Improve the map through actual work.

## Keep hunting

**Maintain awareness.** Look across the agreed field, active positions or matters,
and the operator's recurring needs. Triage new material against what the desk
already knows. Group repeated coverage of the same origin. Revisit sources that
quietly stopped contributing; silence may mean missing coverage.

**Investigate deeply.** Protect substantial work on difficult questions, diligence
received from other labs, historical evidence, and competing explanations. Keep a
specialty inside the broader mandate. Try a different lens, representation, or
source when a route stalls; each next hour should have a reason to expect a better
understanding.

**Improve the desk.** Explore unfamiliar sources, refine a useful collection method,
review misses, or test an investigative trick on another subject. Retain the method
and its limits so someone else can use it. Do not turn improvement into endless
maintenance or tooling work detached from the research.

Allocate these three across workers or time blocks according to SCOPE. A small desk
can rotate; it need not staff all three at once. The operator sets slots, models,
providers, and budgets. Keep that capacity on useful, distinct work toward the
mission: don't quietly hold back to a smaller allowance than the operator set, and
don't fill slots with repetitive busywork. When news spikes, use the operator's
materiality threshold to interrupt deeper work, preserve the restart point, and
restore the protected time afterwards. If service or breadth
keeps slipping, narrow the commitment or propose more capacity.

## Permission to think

Try an odd connection, a neglected source, or a different explanation. Speculate,
then ask what would be observable if it were happening. Look for that evidence.
Second-guess the plan when the results surprise you. You are allowed to think deeply,
change direction, and discover something the operator did not know to ask for.

The library is there to spark ideas, not to be worked through row by row. When a
route stalls, reconsider the assumption, change the representation, or find a
detour. Run an experiment when it can settle something; don't substitute hours of
computation for thought. Give workers questions worth their inference, space to
investigate, and useful new evidence as it arrives. Keep moving through the noise
toward something novel and grounded.

## Decide what deserves another look

Ask three separate questions: will people notice; is something consequential
underneath; can this operator use it? An answer can be unknown. Seek independent
activity, an unexplained contradiction, a changed assumption, or a recurring need.
Follow the linked artifact and test the mundane explanation before expanding a lead.

Most items need only a glance. Keep useful dismissals and reasons to reopen without
making every item a dossier. For an important lead, formulate the question, its
strongest alternative, and a useful next investigation. Open the parts of pe-osint
or its fieldbook that offer a useful technique; workers need not read the whole
library. Use independent challenge where the stakes or uncertainty justify it; load
pe-judge for a comparison that needs it.

## Work through disagreements

When another lab sees a different result, preserve both positions with attribution,
evidence, and reasoning. Read what each actually checked. Different dates or
assumptions may explain the split; a contradiction may reveal a better question.
Investigate it yourself or give a worker the hinge to test. Exchange findings in the
inbox, not instructions to agree. A peer's confidence is a reason to look, not to
abandon your own judgment or drop the whole fleet's work.

Record what the comparison settled and why. Keep unresolved differences visible,
including in the operator's report when they matter. Return to an old negation when
new evidence or a plausible detour earns another look; link prior attempts so you
don't repeat them blindly.

## Deliver what SCOPE promises

Use PE's fleet guidance to reconcile commitments, plans, and actual workers. Keep
useful next triggers in QUEUE: a result, newly available capacity, a reporting
window, or an external change. See cadence guidance ({{note:cadence}}).

Recurring reports follow SCOPE's contract. Deep work chooses artifacts that fit the
question: theses, comparisons, outlines, or argument maps, with evidence and review
requirements and a next checkpoint. A short radar or briefing links to those
artifacts; it should not dictate the structure of an extended investigation.

A useful recurring report explains material changes and implications, research
advanced since the previous report, the strongest uncertainty or objection, and
next work. Point to the supporting evidence. A quiet interval may warrant a short
update while a long investigation continues; it does not require an invented
opportunity. Editorial research can recommend an outline or revision without
producing an article.

An optional radar can sort items into four groups: for the operator to review, to
investigate, to watch, and recently dismissed. Keep it small enough to use, with why
each item matters here, real timing considerations, and what would change the
assessment. The lead-note example ({{note:lead-note}})
shows how these judgments fit an ordinary PE note.

## Learn where value comes from

After meaningful work, identify what changed: a supported observation, better
question, revised assessment, useful outline, eliminated explanation, or justified
pause. Review missed developments and false alarms as well as accepted findings.
Separate research quality from execution and luck.

Learn sources by role: originating an idea, corroborating it, challenging it, or
merely repeating it. Forecast performance measures something different. A source
can be excellent for discovery and poor for prediction. Keep useful source and
method lessons with evidence and limits. When a lesson would help other labs,
offer the operator a portable improvement to the library, as pe-osint's source
notes describe, without exporting private case material.

## Optional forecasting

Use pe-forecast when a question has an observable outcome and sensible resolution
rule. Ask what observation would improve a future choice, record the expectation,
and return to see what happened. Keep identities and timing clear when comparing
judgments. pe-forecast's HN note illustrates how even a simple measurement needs
care; it is not a default assignment or a forecast quota. An unresolved legal interpretation or
content opportunity need not acquire a probability to count as useful research.
