# Investigations that can grow into specialties

Use these plays when a desk needs a stronger research direction. They are imagined
routes through evidence, not substantive claims about a company, treatment, or
legal position. Start with a useful question and follow the route that earns its
next step. The OSINT lenses (pe-osint) supply smaller moves; the catalogs
supply access routes. pe-osint's `notes/fieldbook.plays.md` adds 21 sourced
techniques, including positive-outlier comparison, procurement trails, citation
lineage, and sensor-aware checks. Its `notes/fieldbook.examples.md` shows published
investigations to study when the next useful move is not obvious.

## Follow the constraint, then look for the beneficiary

A mining announcement attracts attention. The interesting question may be which
constraint separates a deposit from usable material and whether another business
controls it. Map the stages: extraction, processing, qualification, transportation,
financing, and customers. Mark each relationship as documented, inferred, or unknown.

Follow company disclosures into named counterparties. Compare plans with operating
assets and commitments. Search the buyer's filings for the same relationship;
one side's description may not establish the other's dependence. Examine timing:
a proposed facility and a customer's near-term requirement may never overlap.
Try the counterthesis that supply arrives but economics accrue somewhere else.

Useful branches include bottleneck maps, competitor comparisons, scenarios for
substitution, and conditional beneficiary theses. Look beyond the first company:
processing, specialized equipment, logistics, and alternative materials may deserve
research. A surprising link earns investigation, not automatic portfolio relevance.

Keep the specialty alive by watching the assumptions that distinguish scenarios.
When financing, capacity, or demand changes, revisit those links. Preserve the
method (trace a headline to its binding constraint) even if the original thesis fails.

## Cross from a scientific result into the system around it

A drug or device development may matter through much more than its headline result.
A financial or editorial desk can investigate the surrounding system without
pretending to make a clinical determination. Separate the research questions:
what the underlying study reports; what remains unestablished; what manufacturing,
distribution, coverage, administrative, or customer constraints might affect use.

Start with the trial record and primary publication, then follow named sponsors,
manufacturers, official guidance, and relevant business disclosures. Read the
study's population and outcome limitations before transferring its implications.
A trial registration, regulatory status, and observed clinical benefit are different
claims. Investigate each required link instead of treating them as one development.

Medical paperwork or practitioner discussions might reveal friction that a market
headline misses. Use lawful public professional material and aggregate needs;
do not build patient profiles. Compare independent accounts and look for official
or organizational evidence of the process involved. Repeated complaints can suggest
a question about administration without establishing prevalence or clinical impact.

The resulting specialty might include a process map, a source-linked explanation,
a set of customer questions, or a conditional commercial scenario. A strong outcome
may be that the proposed connection cannot yet be supported. Maintain the broader
desk's coverage while following the most consequential missing links.

## Find an audience before naming the content pillar

An editorial desk notices questions spread across communities, search phrasing,
product comparisons, and customer objections. Instead of grouping only by shared
keywords, ask what people are trying to decide. Collect the phrasing before imposing
the publication's vocabulary; different audiences can use the same term for
different jobs.

Compare how beginners, practitioners, and purchasers ask about the category. Follow
answers to the sources they rely on and check what they leave unresolved. Read
competitor coverage for its explanatory strengths as well as gaps: a useful
concept may transfer even when the audience or conclusion does not. Test whether
the supposed gap is missing demand, inaccessible expertise, or merely a differently
named answer.

Build a small map of questions, prerequisites, and decisions. This can reveal a
content pillar, comparison series, revision program, or an underserved audience
rather than a queue of isolated posts. For each proposal, name the original
research, demonstration, expertise, or data needed to deserve publication.
A limited sample can test an explanation; it should not substitute for validation.

Across months, compare proposed opportunities with editorial decisions and audience
response. Preserve the source combinations and investigative techniques that found
useful needs, including those that suggested updating existing work. Distinguish a
method that repeatedly discovers good questions from one lucky high-traffic story.

## Investigate both the position and the procedure

A case desk can organize inquiry around propositions and factual prerequisites,
then ask which authority supports each and what could defeat it. Search historical
terms and alternative characterizations, not just the preferred contemporary label.
Follow citations backwards to the original reasoning and forwards to relevant
subsequent treatment where access permits. Record unavailable checks explicitly.

Procedural research asks a different set of questions: which rule/version applies,
what event triggers it, which facts are established, and what consequence the
rule actually supports. Apply that inquiry to both sides. A checklist should be
traceable to the matter; apparent irregularity alone is not a strategy. Let the
attorney validate deadlines, applicability, and any proposed action.

A useful investigation might dismantle the broad position but recover a narrower
argument. Another might show that an attractive procedural route cannot apply.
Both deserve preservation with the factual conditions that mattered. Reusable
methods include searching an older terminology, tracing an exception's origin,
or comparing the two sides against the same prerequisites. Client facts and
strategy remain within their permitted boundary.

## Test a planning idea by changing the facts that sustain it

For tax strategy, start with the proposed treatment and draw out its prerequisites:
entity, jurisdiction, period, sequence, documentation, elections, and exceptions.
Separate the authority's text from the factual configuration to which the desk is
trying to apply it. Compare the strongest adverse interpretation before calculating
an attractive outcome.

Vary one meaningful assumption at a time. Which distinction changes applicability?
Which merely changes the estimate? Which uncertainty prevents any responsible
comparison? Retain calculations with inputs and limits so a changed fact can lead
to a targeted rerun rather than a fresh speculative memo.

A small-business desk might use this method to prepare questions around recurring
transactions. An investor desk might compare prospective sequences. An enterprise
desk might discover that separate research lines assumed incompatible facts.
The output can be a conditional comparison, documentation needs, or a reason to
abandon the idea. Qualified review precedes reliance or implementation.

## Choose the next route without losing the desk

A useful branch has a plausible implication, a way to test it, and a relationship
to the operator's priorities. Maintain a few distinct branches rather than endlessly
polishing the first. Broaden when the missing insight is an alternative; deepen
when the bottleneck is a named factual or analytical question. Stop when available
work cannot usefully change the assessment, with a concrete reason to return.

Other labs' work can supply leads, contrary evidence, or an unfamiliar method.
Check its source dates, assumptions, scope, and permissions before use. Preserve
the transferable trick separately from its case-specific conclusion. This is how
one desk's experience can help another without becoming inherited certainty.
