# Learn where useful research comes from

A desk can improve its knowledge of the world and still keep looking in the wrong
places. Source intelligence asks which routes produce useful questions, reliable
checks, or early warnings, and under what conditions. It belongs beside the
investigative methods, not inside a forecast score.

## Follow the origin, not the volume

When a useful lead arrives, follow its citations and shared wording backwards.
Was it first visible in a local filing, professional discussion, conference paper,
company disclosure, or another desk's original work? Several news articles may be
one observation; an obscure source may deserve credit even when a familiar outlet
made the lead easy to find.

Identify the source's role in that investigation:

| Role | What it contributed | Useful limitation to retain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin | A new question, relationship, or observation | May be unreliable or unrepresentative |
| Corroboration | Independent evidence of the specific claim | Independence and scope still need checking |
| Challenge | An exception, contrary fact, or alternative explanation | Can defeat one claim without establishing another |
| Context | Terminology, chronology, prior attempts, or background | Does not itself establish the current claim |
| Repetition | Repackaged an already known source | Can measure attention without adding evidence |

One source can play several roles. Do not rank them by a single “quality” number.
A community may originate valuable questions but rarely predict their outcomes;
a slow official source may be essential for validation but useless for early warning.

## Test the route on another investigation

Suppose a professional forum repeatedly surfaces administrative friction before
mainstream coverage. Try a narrow comparison: which independently observed problems
appear there, which later receive confirmation, and which are misleading anecdotes?
Record the source combination that made the difference. It might be the forum plus
an official process description, rather than the forum alone.

Do the same for sources that disappoint. Is the problem stale indexing, repeated
press releases, subject mismatch, missing languages, an unreliable access route,
or a weak inference by the desk? A failed thesis does not automatically indict its
originating source. A source that gives prompt adverse evidence can save more time
than a large feed of promising but uncheckable leads.

Compare usefulness with access and review costs. Try a cheaper cadence, narrower
query, different source, or manual read before building a permanent collector.
Improvement should serve investigations and reporting, not become a parallel
catalog-maintenance project.

## Preserve a portable lesson

Keep lab-specific evidence in PE. When a lesson is reusable, propose a small library
update: the research question, method/source, what worked, how it can mislead, and
the evidence for the lesson. Include a counterexample or untested boundary when
known. A few lines in an existing note or source entry are enough; no new dossier
is required for each source visit.

Separate three things:

- **Evidence:** what this lab found in this matter, at these dates and under these facts.
- **Method:** how to investigate a similar question and what would make the method fail.
- **Resource intelligence:** where to look, how access works, and what experience or documentation establishes about its use.

Before sharing, strip private matter details, portfolio conclusions, account access,
and assumptions that do not transfer. A new desk inherits a promising route to try,
not a conclusion to believe. Require permission for a new sharing destination;
a proposal in task scratch does not publish itself.

Use [catalog conventions](catalog.md) for baseline knowledge, material
updates, and verification receipts. Use source/research timestamps for actual
observations; an edited catalog entry is not a fresh check of the world.

## From local lesson to a library change

When a discovery would help another lab, a worker leaves a short proposal in its
own scratch: the target note, what was found, the method and its failure conditions,
shareable evidence (or the route marked untested), and, if useful, a diff against
the shipped note. The manager checks the claim,
transferability, evidence that may be shared, and the exact proposed diff. Reproduce
an access claim on public inputs when possible; private evidence stays in the lab.

These notes ship with Persistent Eclipse, which belongs to the operator. After
review, the manager offers the proposal to the operator rather than editing the
installed notes; workers don't publish proposals. Record the outcome (accepted, or
declined and why) beside the local lesson. A local proposal is useful even while
promotion is deferred.
